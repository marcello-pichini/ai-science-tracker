AI in Astronomy: Exoplanets and Gravitational Lenses

How ExoMiner validated planets in Kepler data and Euclid used AI and human review to select gravitational-lens candidates.

Dossiers

Updated 22 Nov 2026

AI helps astronomers find signals worth following up in large surveys. ExoMiner used Kepler data to support statistical validation of exoplanets. Euclid’s machine-learning rankings guided reviewers to possible gravitational lenses, which specialists then graded using the images.

ExoMiner: statistical validation of Kepler signals

ExoMiner learned to distinguish potential planet transits from false positives using Kepler data and diagnostic measurements. The authors reported statistical validation of 301 planets. That result relied on the statistical evidence in the study. Individual planets can also be investigated through independent observations.

Kepler: how multiple signals change the evidence

A follow-up combined ExoMiner scores with information about the number and types of transit signals in the same system. The authors statistically validated 69 additional planets in systems with multiple Kepler objects of interest. This is a follow-up from the same research program.

Euclid: finding candidate gravitational lenses

Euclid’s search combined machine-learning rankings, volunteer screening and expert grading. Its Quick Release 1 study reported 497 strong-lens candidates, including 250 assigned the highest grade. Those grades express confidence based on the available images. Follow-up observations and lens modelling can provide further evidence for individual candidates.

How should readers compare the counts?

A screened candidate is a signal selected for scrutiny. Statistical validation depends on a stated false-positive assessment. An expert grade reflects judgement under a particular review protocol. Each count belongs to the survey and evidence standard that produced it.

How to interpret the evidence

What an ExoMiner benchmark can tell us

A dip in a star’s brightness can come from a planet passing in front of it. An eclipsing binary star, a nearby source or an instrument artefact can produce a similar signature. ExoMiner combines different diagnostics, helping with the vetting step after a survey has found a possible transit signal.

The comparison at fixed precision asks how many labelled examples each classifier can recover while keeping the selected set similarly clean. The 17.3 percentage-point difference is specific to the reported test. The separate set of 301 statistical validations concerns previously unvalidated Kepler objects selected under the study’s criteria.

Why several periodic signals change the assessment

Several transit-like signals associated with one star carry context that a classifier looking at a single signal can miss. The multiplicity study used the number and status of signals in the system together with the classifier’s score. This allowed the authors to test whether system-level information made the existing screen more informative.

The 69 reported validations come from systems with multiple Kepler objects of interest. The same statistical boost cannot be assumed for isolated signals or transferred to another survey without checking its false positives and how it groups signals by host star.

ExoMiner multiplicity study

Can Euclid’s ranking miss convincing lenses?

Euclid asks a different question of its images. Light from a background galaxy can be bent around a foreground galaxy, producing arcs or multiple images. The pipeline used machine learning to prioritise cutouts, then volunteers and experts inspected them. The 497 selected candidates include 250 grade A and 247 grade B entries.

To examine what ranking missed, Euclid also showed volunteers a random sample of about 40,000 sources. The authors estimated that the model-prioritised search recovered around 60% of the candidates an exhaustive visual review could have found. That estimate describes the Q1 selection procedure. Independent observations can add evidence about individual lenses.
