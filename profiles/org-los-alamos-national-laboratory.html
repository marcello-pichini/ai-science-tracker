1 Mar 2023Los Alamos National Laboratory; Argonne National Laboratory

Adaptive learning finds higher-activity Fe–N–C oxygen-reduction catalysts

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: An adaptive-learning design-of-experiments loop guided robotic high-throughput synthesis of Fe–N–C oxygen-reduction electrocatalysts across a six-dimensional process/composition space. Starting from 36 characterized samples, the team synthesized 14 additional catalyst formulations from a nominal search space of roughly 10 billion combinations. Four outperformed the best sample in the initial dataset; the best showed about 33% higher ORR activity, reported as 16.3 mA mg⁻¹ at 0.8 V in rotating-disk measurements.

Technology: Adaptive learning framework with machine-learning surrogates, statistical inference, uncertainty quantification, and global optimization, paired with robotic high-throughput synthesis

Editorial relevance 53 materials Original sourcePaper Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: direct test. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
The model-based design-of-experiments tool used current measurements and uncertainty to select subsequent synthesis conditions in a six-dimensional search space. It guided the selection of 14 samples for synthesis and measurement, producing four catalysts that exceeded the initial best. Robotic high-throughput synthesis and RRDE testing generated the experimental feedback; researchers interpreted the results.
Test and result
An adaptive-learning design-of-experiments loop guided robotic high-throughput synthesis of Fe–N–C oxygen-reduction electrocatalysts across a six-dimensional process/composition space.
Limitation / still unknown
The finding concerns optimized Fe–N–C catalyst formulations and synthesis conditions within the study’s defined six-dimensional search; it is not discovery of a new catalyst family or proof that all approximately 10 billion combinations were tested. The 33% improvement is relative to the best sample in the initial 36-sample dataset. Activity was measured using rotating-ring disk electrode testing, not a fuel-cell device or long-duration operating demonstration. The study reports 14 new synthesized catalysts and four that beat the initial samples; these are one paper-level result, not separate entries.

10 Nov 2021Los Alamos National Laboratory; École Normale Supérieure; University of California, Santa Cruz and collaborators

Deep learning extends the mapped extent of a North Anatolian Fault slow-slip event

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: A deep convolutional autoencoder trained on synthetic deformation and atmospheric-noise time series recovered millimeter-scale ground motion from real InSAR without being given the fault location. Applied to COSMO-SkyMed observations of the 2013 North Anatolian Fault slow-slip event, it detected about 2 mm of slip beyond the previously identified segment and revised the estimated along-strike event length from about 10 km to 15–20 km. The same paper used the approach to resolve transient millimeter-scale subsidence at California’s Coso geothermal field. This is one paper-level finding enabled by the model’s extraction of deformation signals from noisy satellite data.

Technology: Deep convolutional autoencoder trained on synthetic InSAR time series

Editorial relevance 47 physics Original sourcePaper Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: retrospective or benchmark. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
The deep autoencoder separated persistent ground deformation patterns from atmospheric noise in real InSAR data without prior fault-location information, resolving the additional 2 mm slow-slip segment; the model was trained on synthetic data and was not fine-tuned on the real case.
Test and result
A deep convolutional autoencoder trained on synthetic deformation and atmospheric-noise time series recovered millimeter-scale ground motion from real InSAR without being given the fault location.
Limitation / still unknown
The newly resolved extension is a reanalysis of an existing 2013 InSAR event, not a newly occurring earthquake. The paper’s reported extension is model-derived; the original expert analysis had detected the larger slow-slip segment, while the autoencoder identifies an additional lower-amplitude portion. The model assumes atmospheric noise characteristics represented by its synthetic training data and omits some noise sources, including ionospheric perturbations. Coso results are a separate application within the same paper and are not counted separately.
