19 Jun 2026University of Pennsylvania

Deep learning identifies antimicrobial peptides hidden in prion-related proteins

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: APEX 1.1 screened 19.3 million peptide fragments from 2,897 prion-related proteins and identified 1,179 candidate antimicrobial peptides, termed prionins. Among 75 synthesized candidates, 59 inhibited at least one tested bacterial strain; two selected peptides reduced Acinetobacter baumannii burden in a mouse skin-abscess model. The study shows that prion-related sequences can yield experimentally active antimicrobial fragments, but does not show that these peptides are naturally released or function as host-defense agents in their source organisms.

Technology: APEX 1.1 deep-learning antimicrobial-peptide prediction and prioritization

12 Jul 2025University of Pennsylvania

Deep learning mines venom proteomes for antimicrobial peptides active in vitro and in a mouse infection model

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: The APEX deep-learning model screened 16,123 venom proteins by generating and scoring 40,626,260 venom-encrypted peptides. After activity prediction and filtering for low similarity to known antimicrobial peptides, the authors selected 58 for experiments; 53 showed antimicrobial activity against at least one tested pathogen. Three selected peptides reduced bacterial counts after topical treatment in a mouse Acinetobacter baumannii skin-abscess model; the wolf-spider-derived Arachnoserver-5 peptide produced a reported three-log reduction versus untreated controls through day four, without significant body-weight change under the study conditions.

Technology: APEX deep-learning model for bacterial strain-specific minimum inhibitory concentration prediction

Editorial relevance 60 medicine Published articlePublished article Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: direct test. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
APEX scored 40.6 million candidate peptide fragments generated from venom proteins for predicted antimicrobial activity. The authors filtered predicted hits for sequence dissimilarity to known antimicrobial peptides, synthesized a selected subset, and experimentally tested antimicrobial activity, cytotoxicity and selected animal-model efficacy. The AI model narrowed an otherwise very large peptide search space; assays established which selected candidates were active.
Test and result
After activity prediction and filtering for low similarity to known antimicrobial peptides, the authors selected 58 for experiments; 53 showed antimicrobial activity against at least one tested pathogen.
Limitation / still unknown
Only 58 of the 386 low-similarity candidates were experimentally tested, and activity was measured against a defined panel of 11 strains; some tested peptides were cytotoxic. The mouse study was a topical single-dose skin-abscess model with six animals per group, not systemic treatment or human efficacy. The paper notes possible ion-channel activity for many candidates and calls for follow-up safety testing. A related APEX study mined archaeal proteomes (10.1038/s41564-025-02061-0); this entry is limited to the separate venom-protein source library and its distinct peptide set and experiments.

5 Jul 2021University of Pennsylvania

Simulated low-field MRI retains pathology-detection signals with reduced small-lesion sensitivity

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: First posted in July 2021 and journal-published that December, this study used DenseNet-121 classifiers to compare pathology detection in 3T MRI and simulated 64mT-quality images from glioma, stroke and multiple-sclerosis datasets. Patient-separated tests reported similar slice-level AUCs, including 0.97 versus 0.98 for low-grade glioma, while simulated low-field sensitivity declined for smaller lesions. The transformation had limited paired-image and radiologist checks, but omitted field-dependent tissue contrast and device artifacts; these simulations do not establish diagnostic equivalence or clinical performance of an actual low-field scanner.

Technology: DenseNet-121 convolutional neural network for pathology detection

Editorial relevance 40 medicine Published articlePublished article Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: retrospective or benchmark. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
DenseNet-121, initialized with ImageNet weights, learns lesion-present versus lesion-absent image classification. Comparing held-out original and simulated datasets probes a specific imaging-performance question; the image transformation itself was conventional rather than learned.
Test and result
A non-neural image transformation was fitted using three hydrocephalus patients and checked in three additional patients; three radiologists compared paired real/simulated images from ten MS patients. DenseNet-121 classifiers were trained separately on original/simulated images for low-grade glioma (76 subjects), high-grade glioma (259), stroke (28) and MS (20), using approximately 9:1 patient-separated train/test splits. Reported slice-level AUCs were respectively 0.97/0.98, 0.96/0.95, 0.94/0.94 and 0.90/0.87.
Limitation / still unknown
The simulation approximates resolution/noise but not all field-strength contrast or device-specific artifacts. Separate disease classifiers perform simple lesion detection, not differential diagnosis or full radiologic assessment. Small test cohorts and degraded detection of small lesions limit generalization; prospective actual-device trials and patient benefit were not established.
