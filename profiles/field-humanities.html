2 Nov 2026Vesuvius Challenge

Writing seen in PHerc. 306B for the first time in 2000 years

Vesuvius Challenge awarded a $50K First Letters Prize for scroll PHerc. 306B, where the winning submission reached the 10-character target and the papyrology team agreed on a reading, tentatively a Greek phrase with parallels in Aristotle and Philodemus. Ink was detected by multiplying two models (TRACE v3B and a fine-tuned A-hn). Letters remain faint, the readable area is small, and the work relied on hundreds of hours of manual labeling.

Technology: ML ink-detection models (TRACE v3B, A-hn) with VC3D GrowPatch

A Herculaneum scroll read end to end without opening

Machine-learning ink detection on synchrotron scans let papyrologists read about 22 columns of a likely Stoic ethics text from scroll PHerc. 1667. The text is fragmentary because only the inner core survives, and humans transcribed and reviewed every reading.

27 Apr 2026University of New Mexico; New Mexico Consortium; Utah State University; Louisiana State University

Simulated training data locates archaeological tar-kiln-like sites in Louisiana, field check finds WWII features

Machine learning models trained on simulated landscape data flagged 9 of 12 known targets (model 1) and all 12 (model 2, with 686 false positives among 709 remaining predictions) in Kisatchie National Forest, Louisiana; a model trained on real South Carolina tar kilns found 11 of 12. Field visits to 11 targets and augering of 2 found none of the expected tar-kiln features, and the authors concluded the structures more likely relate to a WWII military training site. Evidence caveat: only 12 known targets, and field validation is qualitative.

Technology: Machine learning classifiers trained on simulated archaeological sites (with a real tar-kiln-trained comparison model)

23 Jul 2025Google DeepMind

Aeneas: restoration, dating, and provenance of Latin inscriptions

The model restores texts with 73% top-20 accuracy for gaps up to ten characters (58% when length is unknown), attributes provenance with 72% accuracy across 62 Roman provinces, and dates inscriptions within 13 years; it was trained on over 176,000 inscriptions. In a study with 23 historians on 60 inscriptions, results were best when parallels and predictions were used together. Published in Nature.

Technology: Aeneas

17 Jul 2025University of Bologna

AI-guided CORONA imagery search finds four archaeologically verified tells in Abu Ghraib

A fine-tuned deep-learning model trained with 1960s CORONA satellite images flagged eight previously unrecognized tell candidates in Iraq's Abu Ghraib district. Archaeologists investigated all eight; four contained surface ceramic sherds confirming archaeological remains and enabling dating. The AI suggestions led the field team to inspect locations that routine surveys would not have visited.

Technology: MANet deep convolutional segmentation network with attention, transfer learning and two-stage fine-tuning

4 Jun 2025University of Groningen; Pisa, KU Leuven, and University of Southern Denmark

Dead Sea Scrolls redated with AI and radiocarbon

The Enoch tool dated the writing of 135 manuscripts with about 79% agreement with palaeographers and roughly 30-year precision, following new radiocarbon dating; many scrolls appear older than expected. The 79% measures agreement with palaeographers, not accuracy against known dates; the announcement gives no details on training data.

26 Mar 2025Leiden University; University of Toronto; University of Colorado Boulder

Deep learning maps Angkorian-period reservoirs beyond Greater Angkor

A DeepLabV3+ segmentation model trained on about 3,600 high-quality samples from over 11,000 candidates mapped Angkor-period reservoirs in satellite imagery across the wider Angkorian realm. The best F1 score reached 45% in favourable regions, comparable to a trained human interpreter. The authors present the model as a second opinion for landscape archaeologists that requires human validation.

Technology: DeepLabV3+ semantic segmentation

For the first time, a complete word (‘porphyras’, purple) was read from inside a never-opened carbonized scroll; a model trained on crack patterns yielded 13 letters independently and unanimously annotated by papyrologists. The letters are faint and lack context; other readings are not ruled out.
