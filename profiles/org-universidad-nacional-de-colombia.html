Machine learning maps modeled fault-system CO₂ flux within active tectonic regions worldwide

A stacked ensemble trained on 867 compiled degassing records and geospatial raster predictors generated a continuous 0.10° map of model-estimated CO₂ flux within an active-tectonics applicability mask. The authors report spatially coherent high-flux estimates along the Andes, Pacific Ring of Fire and other active orogens. This is a new model-derived spatial estimate for fault-system flux, rather than a direct global survey or measurement.

Technology: Stacked regression ensemble combining support-vector, k-nearest-neighbor, decision-tree, random-forest and gradient-boosting models

Evidence context (descriptions follow the cited sources; test type is a catalog classification):

  • Publication status: Peer-reviewed paper
  • Test type: retrospective or benchmark
  • Tested: A stacked ensemble trained on 867 compiled degassing records and geospatial raster predictors generated a continuous 0.10° map of model-estimated CO₂ flux within an active-tectonics applicability mask.
  • Still unknown: Predictions are model estimates based on 867 compiled records with heterogeneous dates and measurement techniques, not direct measurements at every pixel. The mask is limited to areas within 40 km of mapped active faults or with PGA ≥0.10 g; the 0.10° raster smooths local geology. The abstract reports R²=0.677, while the full text gives stacking test R²=0.693 and validation R²=0.552; fit is moderate. Sampling gaps include central Africa, eastern Asia and North America. High-flux belts overlap known tectonic/geothermal regions, and SHAP associations do not establish causal controls.
  • AI role: Five regression learners were tuned and combined in a stacking regressor; that model predicted flux pixel by pixel from global raster inputs and generated the paper’s continuous map of estimated fault-system flux. The mask excluded areas outside the model’s stated tectonically active applicability domain.
