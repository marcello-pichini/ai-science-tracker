4 Nov 2026Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai; Mount Sinai Morningside Hospital; The Mount Sinai Hospital; NYC Health + Hospitals

Deployed delirium model retains discrimination at a second hospital

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: A frozen random-forest system combining clinical data and an NLP risk score was evaluated during deployment at a second Mount Sinai hospital. Retrospective workflow analysis reported AUC 0.91 and positive predictive value 25.9%; targeted assessments had a higher delirium yield than a historical period. Selective verification and changing workflows prevent a causal patient-benefit interpretation.

Technology: Random forest with structured clinical features and an NLP-derived risk score

Editorial relevance 40 medicine Published article Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: retrospective or benchmark. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
A trained random forest fused structured/semistructured clinical information with an NLP-derived note score to prioritize live clinical delirium assessments; the study measured its within-system transportability and operational assessment yield.
Test and result
Frozen model deployed without local retraining at Mount Sinai Morningside in November 2024. Retrospective analysis compared 9,020 admissions there with 21,186 contemporaneous development-hospital admissions and 2,889 historical predeployment admissions. At the second hospital, reported AUC was 0.91 and positive predictive value 25.9%. Only 2,649 of 9,020 admissions received CAM assessments; the rest relied on diagnosis codes. Mean monthly yield among CAM assessments rose from 9.23% to 19.59%.
Limitation / still unknown
Same-health-system transportability, not independent-health-system validation. Model-guided CAM assessment creates incomplete verification and incorporation biases; remaining reference labels use diagnosis codes. Site thresholds differ, calibration was not the primary endpoint and false positives were frequent. Historical2018–2019 versus2024–2025 comparisons are confounded and measure yield per assessment, not incidence or improved outcomes. Linked invited commentary emphasizes these interpretation limits.

9 Jul 2025Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai; Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center; Technical University of Munich; Sahlgrenska University Hospital and University of Gothenburg; SUNY Upstate Medical University; University of Montreal Hospital Centre

Prospective silent-trial validation of a fine-tuned pathology foundation model for EGFR mutation screening in lung adenocarcinoma

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: A fine-tuned Prov-GigaPath ViT-g model, named EAGLE, predicted EGFR mutation status from routine H&E slides plus clinical features in lung adenocarcinoma. Across an international dataset of 8,461 slides it achieved mean AUC 0.847 internally and 0.870 in external cohorts. In a four-month prospective silent trial on 197 primary lung-adenocarcinoma samples, EAGLE achieved AUC 0.890. Simulated AI-assisted triage thresholds reduced rapid molecular EGFR tests by up to 43% while maintaining the study’s performance criteria.

Technology: EAGLE, a task-specific model fine-tuned from the Prov-GigaPath ViT-g pathology foundation model, combining H&E whole-slide image features with clinical variables for EGFR mutation prediction

Editorial relevance 60 medicine Published articlePublished article Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: prospective or independent check. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
The AI model predicted tumor EGFR mutation status directly from routine H&E digital slides, using sequencing results as ground truth. Fine-tuning the pathology foundation model produced cross-institution validation, and real-time inference in a prospective silent trial tested generalization on newly processed clinical slides. The model score was evaluated as a screening/triage layer alongside rapid molecular testing; it did not replace genomic sequencing.
Test and result
Across an international dataset of 8,461 slides it achieved mean AUC 0.847 internally and 0.870 in external cohorts.
Limitation / still unknown
The four-month prospective trial was silent: AI outputs did not guide care or replace standard testing. The 43% reduction is a simulated triage workflow at a selected score threshold, not an observed reduction in tests or evidence of improved outcomes. The cohort was 197 primary cases at one real-time deployment site, while broader retrospective/external cohorts supported generalization. EAGLE predicts presence of EGFR mutation but does not identify the mutation subtype; sequencing remains necessary before treatment. Performance was lower on metastatic samples. The paper is a distinct 2025 model iteration and validation study in the lineage of the group’s 2022 arXiv proof of concept (10.48550/arXiv.2206.10573); the prior work is cited as antecedent, not treated as a second alias or hidden as the first-ever pathology-image EGFR result.

29 Dec 2023Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai

Deep learning ECG model estimates right ventricular size and function

Source check: Source checked (abstract only). Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: A deep learning ECG model was trained against cardiac MRI in UK Biobank (n = 42,938), fine-tuned in Mount Sinai Hospital data (n = 3019) and prospectively validated over 4 months (n = 115). AUROCs for RV dysfunction were 0.86, 0.81 and 0.77, and for RV dilation 0.91, 0.81 and 0.92 across the three cohorts. RVEF prediction was associated with transplant-free survival. The prospective set is small and RV dysfunction prevalence was low in UK Biobank (1.0%).

Technology: Deep learning model on 12-lead ECG predicting RV dilation, RV dysfunction, RVEF and RVEDV against cardiac MRI

25 Apr 2023Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai; University of Maryland School of Medicine; Hospital for Special Surgery

Longitudinal EHR machine learning identifies patients who may need autoimmune rheumatic-disease evaluation years earlier

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: A random-forest model trained on longitudinal electronic health records from 161,584 people across two institutions identified records associated with high-specificity autoantibody testing for systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARDs). The model achieved AUROC 0.93 in internal validation and 0.87 on the external All of Us cohort. In a separate BioMe cohort, model probabilities tracked SARD and other autoimmune diagnoses; records could predict later autoantibody testing and rheumatology encounters up to five years before those events. The study also found higher autoantibody yield among model-prioritized individuals than under the comparison clinical-testing rate.

Technology: Random-forest machine-learning model using longitudinal electronic-health-record diagnoses, medications, laboratory results and vital signs

Editorial relevance 47 medicine Published articlePublished article Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: retrospective or benchmark. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
The model ranked patients by probability of receiving SARD-specific autoantibody testing, using clinical data preceding the test or rheumatology encounter. Investigators evaluated performance in internal validation, the external All of Us cohort and an independent BioMe cohort, and tested whether higher scores tracked diagnosed autoimmune disease and identified later testing or encounters earlier in retrospective records.
Test and result
A random-forest model trained on longitudinal electronic health records from 161,584 people across two institutions identified records associated with high-specificity autoantibody testing for systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARDs).
Limitation / still unknown
The model predicts clinical suspicion and need for rheumatological testing, not a SARD diagnosis. The study was retrospective and opportunistic; autoantibody testing and rheumatology encounters are imperfect proxies for clinical need, and some diseases were not included in the training target. The authors state prospective studies are needed to determine whether model-guided testing improves care or outcomes. The five-year lead is retrospective prediction of later evaluation events, not proven earlier diagnosis or patient benefit.

31 Oct 2022Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai

CNN-measured tau tangle burden is linked to cognitive impairment in PART

Source check: Source checked (abstract only). Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: In 706 post-mortem brains from elderly people with mild or no amyloid plaques, a convolutional neural network trained to segment neurofibrillary tangles on p-tau-stained hippocampal sections gave tangle counts, positive pixel density and a graph-theory spatial metric. Several of these metrics predicted antemortem cognitive impairment in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex (p < 0.0001); controlling for age, tangle counts remained significant (p = 0.04). Braak stage did not predict impairment. This is a retrospective association, and CNN validation details are not in the abstract.

Technology: Convolutional neural network segmenting neurofibrillary tangles on p-tau stained hippocampus sections

Editorial relevance 40 medicine Published article Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: retrospective or benchmark. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
A CNN segmented tangles to give quantitative burden metrics beyond Braak staging.
Test and result
Retrospective autopsy cohort of 706 brains compared with antemortem cognitive status.
Limitation / still unknown
CNN accuracy against pathologists is not stated in the abstract; association only; full text not reviewed.

30 Oct 2021Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai; University of Sydney

Digital kidney-biopsy damage features are associated with later graft loss

Source check: Source checked. Not independent scientific confirmation.

Finding: Deep-learning image analysis quantified tissue damage in 789 kidney-transplant biopsy slides from two cohorts. A damage score from 12-month biopsies was associated with subsequent graft loss in both cohorts. Baseline-score survival association was not confirmed in the smaller Australian cohort, and clinical utility remains untested.

Technology: Mask R-CNN and U-Net histopathology analysis

Editorial relevance 33 medicine Published articlePublished article Suggest a correction

Publication: Peer-reviewed paper · Test context: retrospective or benchmark. Catalog categories, not an evidence grade.

AI contribution
Neural tissue/immune-cell segmentation supplied quantitative damage features, which ordinary survival analysis related to subsequent graft outcomes.
Test and result
Networks were developed on 60 selected biopsy slides and tested on 33 others. The pipeline then analyzed 478 baseline and 311 twelve-month slides from 404 GoCAR and 212 AUSCAD participants. In 200 GoCAR and 111 AUSCAD twelve-month biopsies, composite tissue/inflammation scores were associated with later death-censored graft loss; thresholds were derived in GoCAR.
Limitation / still unknown
This is retrospective image/outcome analysis of previously collected cohorts, not prospective evaluation of a frozen clinical tool. GoCAR supplies both selected model-development slides and outcome analyses, with exact patient overlap not separated here. Baseline ITAS survival association was not confirmed in AUSCAD. Observational associations, selected thresholds, limited graft-loss events and cohort differences prevent causal, treatment-benefit or universal surrogate-endpoint claims.
