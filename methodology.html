How we select and verify entries

This site tracks scientific results achieved with AI since 2020, starting from official announcements by the organizations that achieved them.

What we include

  • A concrete result in a scientific field (biology, medicine, mathematics, climate, and others), announced by the organization that achieved it.
  • An official page with a date and figures: lab and company blogs (DeepMind, Google Research, Microsoft Research, Meta AI, OpenAI, Anthropic, and others), or announcements from universities and institutes (such as MIT).
  • Tools and models only when accompanied by a verifiable result (a structure, compound, proof, or field test).

What we exclude

  • Product launches and benchmark comparisons without a scientific result. Model releases are tracked separately on the Models page and do not count as discoveries.
  • Awards and recognition (such as the 2024 Nobel Prize): these judge a discovery; they are not the discovery itself.
  • Newsletters, aggregators, and third-party articles as the primary source.
  • Announcements without results yet (funding, collaborations, interviews).

The models page

The Models page answers a different question: which model families are linked by name to catalog discoveries. It lists releases from leading labs since 2020, plus models from any date linked to a discovery.

We do not collect model names, labs, dates, domains, parameter counts, or access information ourselves. They come from the database Data on AI models from Epoch AI, republished under CC BY 4.0. Their stated limitations apply: the list is not exhaustive, and compute and parameter figures are estimates.

We make model-to-discovery links by name: we compare the technology field and each discovery title with model and family names. A match indicates the likely family, not necessarily the exact version used in an experiment. A discovery made with a model that the source does not name will not appear as linked. Some catalog technologies, such as those developed within a single study, have no Epoch AI record; the family can still appear without linked releases.

AI-discovered vulnerabilities

The separate Vuln discoveries page includes publicly disclosed individual vulnerabilities only when original sources establish an AI contribution. It does not include every security news item or convert aggregate bug counts into individual findings. These records are excluded from the scientific-discovery count.

Discovery context, public disclosure, AI attribution and documented remediation are distinct. A previously unknown pre-release bug is not automatically an exploited zero-day. Zero-day context remains attributed to the cited account, and documented fixes do not establish deployment or current safety. No attack is inferred from a vulnerability announcement.

What “Source checked” means

The stored verification status “verified” is displayed as “Source checked.” We have read the official page and checked that the title, date, organization, and figures in the summary match it. This does not mean others have independently confirmed the result: figures are reported by sources and are often “up to” values or results from controlled conditions. The summary reports limits stated by the source (lab-only, mice-only, not clinically validated, or preprint).

An entry marked “needs verification” has a source that has not been read in full, or contains conflicting information.

Evidence context (pilot)

A small pilot adds an optional “Evidence context” to the source details of 12 entries: publication status, what was tested, what the sources say is still unknown, and what the AI system did. Every statement comes from pages that were read for the annotation, which are listed in the data file; nothing is inferred from the website, keywords or the relevance score, and it does not change any score. Where a page could not be read, the field says “Not reported”. It is not a validation grade.

Relevance score

Each entry has a score from 0 to 100: the average of three criteria, each scored from 1 to 5, multiplied by 20. This is an editorial judgment, not an objective measure. It helps sort entries and compare categories; hover over a score to see its three criteria.

Criterion135
Impactniche resultuseful to a field or defined populationchanges how an entire field works
Validationcomputational only or announcementconfirmed in a lab, in the field, or on limited casesindependent evaluation, broad use, or external recognition
Noveltyincremental improvementfirst result of its kind in a fieldparadigm shift

Validation uses the intermediate steps as follows: 4 means evidence in animals, a prospective or randomized clinical study, or experimental confirmation of a predicted structure; 5 also covers routine operational use by the organization responsible, such as a national forecasting centre or a standard software library. Operational use shows that a method is relied on, not that every claim in the announcement holds.

A category average is the mean score of its entries. With only one or two entries, the average is not very informative.

Reading the catalog statistics

The annual chart offers the number of catalog entries and their mean editorial relevance. Means use the rounded 0–100 scores displayed on individual entries, then round the annual average to an integer. Annual sample sizes appear in the table; small samples and the incomplete current year require caution. These are descriptions of a selected collection, not worldwide discovery rates or measures of scientific progress.

The source composition counts each entry by its primary source type, not by evidence quality. “Most recent catalog addition” uses the addition date; it does not indicate the last scientific review or follow-up check.

How we choose the date

We use the date of the first official announcement. If a journal publication comes later, it remains in the summary. When a page’s date conflicts with its text, we use the date consistent with the facts and note the discrepancy (for example, DeepMind’s post about the first 350,000 AlphaFold Database structures is dated 2022 but describes events in 2021).

Reuse and citation

The catalog content (records, summaries and editorial scores) is licensed under CC BY 4.0; please credit “AI Science Tracker” with a link. Epoch AI model data keeps its own CC BY 4.0 attribution, and each linked source keeps its own terms. The repository’s citation file describes the catalog, not the underlying papers.

Limitations

  • Collection is manual and not exhaustive; selection depends on the sources reviewed so far.
  • Companies describe their own results, so the tone may be promotional and claims are not independently assessed.
  • Entries come from official announcements, so most sources are the websites of the organizations that did the work. The catalog therefore reflects what organizations choose to announce; the home page states how concentrated the sources are.
  • Organizations listed together (for example, two labs) are counted under the first one named in charts.

Each entry links to the original announcement. Figures are reported by sources and have not been independently verified.
